23. Camp Prison

The camp prison, known as the ‘bunker’, was completed in 1940.
It consisted of 33 cells. Detention – often made more severe by imprisonment in a blackout cell combined with withdrawal of rations – was one of the official punishments the camp commandant was entitled to impose. Often he did this arbitrarily. Punishment was handed out for the violation of any number of regulations and prohibitions, some of which were unknown to the prisoners and which often contradicted one another.
The bunker was also used to house prisoners who had been transferred to Mauthausen for execution. Some were shot in the camp prison courtyard, others murdered in the gas chamber or execution room.
Interrogations carried out by the SS and Gestapo in the bunker always went hand in hand with torture and severe mistreatment.
Josef Drexel, a German, remembers being in a cell in the bunker:
‘Like all the other cells it was completely empty – except a bucket to relieve yourself. […]
The walls and doors, covered with prisoners’ helpless confessions…
You weren’t always alone in these cells. Often you had company, even if only for a few hours or days […]. Mostly around evening, but often in the night as well, the door would open and someone would be shoved in, stumbling, to become your cellmate for a time, until they had learned to stand up and walk again. Usually the new arrival, feverish and in a terrible state, would immediately stretch out on their stomach in a corner, wordless and groaning.’
