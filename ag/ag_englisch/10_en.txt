10. Prisoner Barrack
Each barracks in the prisoners’ camp was intended for 300 prisoners but, during the second half of the war, some barracks were being used to house up to 2000 people. They were divided into two rooms, A and B, each of which was divided into a sleeping area and a common room. The latter was reserved for the small number of prisoner functionaries. As a rule, two or sometimes more prisoners had to share one bed on the wooden bunks. Often sheets and straw sacks were only provided for the prisoner functionaries. Everyone else usually had little more than an old woollen blanket. Daily life in the camp was shaped in equal measure by frenzied activity and monotony.
Hans Maršálek from Vienna writes:
‘In the main camp and in nearly all the branch camps, the prisoners were woken up by a bell. From spring to autumn at 4.45am, in winter at 5.45am. After the wake-up bell you had to get up at once and then, every day, the same hectic sequence of events repeated itself: The straw sacks had to be smoothed with boards, the edges and corners straightened, and the blankets folded exactly. You had to line up for the toilet and washroom, wash quickly and dress, line up again for soup or coffee, after that clean out your bowl and put everything in its allotted place in the locker. Then you lined up in front of the barracks to be marched to roll call. All this took place amid shoves and blows, accompanied by commands from the block functionaries like … “raus, schnell, bistro, rapido, ausrichten, marsch” etc. …’
